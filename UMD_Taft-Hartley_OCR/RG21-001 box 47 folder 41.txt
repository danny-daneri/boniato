This week, as the 80th Congress convened on Capitol Hill for its second
session, Organized Labor entered a new year--and an election year at that--faced
with the obstacles and uncertainties of the dangerously restrictive and, in many
respects, unconstitutional Taft-Hartley Act.
A review of the history of legislative developments since V-j Day in August
of 1945, will demonstrate how monopoly forces have successfully carried out an anti-
labor program. (This writer hopes that he will be forgiven for retelling this story
by referring to his own published reports in California labor papers describing this
"Big Steal Formula" as it emerged.)
The 79th Congress, Ist Session
Senator Joseph Ball of Minnesota, (Republican advocate of the N.A.M. anti-
labor line and chairman of the Joint Committee on Labor-Management Relations created
by the Taft-Hartley Act) fired the opening gun of the postwar attack on American
trade unionism even before the Japanese surrendered.
On June 20, 1945, the Ball-Burton-Hatch "Industrial Peace" bill was intro-
duced in the Senate of the United States--the brainchild of a so-called "Committee
to Promote Industrial Peace" headed by ex-New Dealer Donald R. Richberg, who de-
serted his labor union clients for the large legal fees of national corporations.
===== PAGE 2 =====
Series of three Richberg artieles adrocating this forerunner of the Taft-Hartley
Act, (containing restrictions on union security and prohibiting so-called "anfair
lebor prectices" by unions) was printed in the Congressional Reeord of July 17, 1945
at Senator Ball'e request.
Early in September of 1945, California labor papers published my artiele
warning that "The Ball-Burton-Hatch bill in a booby-trap placed in the path of
organized Labor on the road back to effective peace-time collective bargaining."
"The fight against Fascism in Europe and Asia has been won," the article
read, "but the battle against the 'Ball-and-Chain' B111 10 a continuation of the
sane struggle against bigoted and anti-denocratie foes of free men and women."
At that early date it was my intention to expose the NAM program designed
to create a pro-employer Federal labor policy under which "the protection of the
National Labor Relations Act, and the Norris Is-Guardia Anti-Injunction Law will
be destroyed; the right to strike and picket will be restricted, and in place of
free collective bargaining, Labor will face an endless chain of briefs, govern-
mental hearings and court proceedinga." I urged trade unionists to write their
Congressnen demanding defeat of this bill "or any bill resembling its 'union-
busting' provisions."
Another such article on September 28, 1945 told how "those Senators and
Representatives who pull for the National Association of Manufacturers and other
organized employer groups were "seeking to cripple the enforcement machinery of
the Wage and Hour Law." It urged that "The N.A.M. attack on the Fair Labor Standards
Act must be met and dealt with in a decisive manner," -- that "a strong effort must
be exerted by the ranks of labor to....offset the pressure of organized merchants
and manufacturers."

The 19th Congress, 2d Session
"The Legislative Offensive - Labor's Best Defense" was published on January
19, 1946, describing the three-point campaign into which "both nationally and
locally, the Merchants' and Manufacturers' Associations have poured millions of
dollars.":
(1) "Introduction of vicious anti-labor bills in Congress and
the state legislatures as well as initiative petitions."
(2) "Issuance of false propaganda to the effect that 'Labor
is in the saddle now just as Big Business was in the 20's.
Unions are rich and powerful and no longer need goverrment
protection from the disadvantages of unequal bargaining
power. ' "
(3) "Exploitation of... reconversion period... shortages to con-
demn Organized Labor for all strikes, and subject unions
to bitter editorial criticism."
The conclusion was offered back in January, 1946 that-
"Now that the postwar period has begun it is time to take
stock... Now is the time to begin planning. Remember this
year and 1940 are both election years. Labor should gain
the political offensive, bearing that fact in mind."
A few weeks later, the House of Representatives passed the "Case Bill",
a proposed labor disputes act with cooling-off periods, injunctions against picket-
ing and boycotting, damage suits against unions, exclusion of supervisors from
Wagner Act protection, and the like. Unionists were beseeched on February 15, 1946
in the California labor press to "Help plead Labor's case against the Case bill."
Extended hearings in the Senate Committee on Education and Labor were held
all during February on the Case bill, followed by two weeks debate on the Senate

Then, the General Motors and Steel strike nettlements seemed to provide a
breathing spell during which the unions might gather their forces.
The Railroad strike of 1946 gave the NAM the opportunity it was waiting for
to catch Labor off-guard. Congress was called into joint session, and the President
was pressured into calling for strike-breaking powers.
Even after the Railroad strike was settled, the Senate passed the Case bill
by almost 2 to 1 on May 25, 1946 and the House voted passage by an even heaviar
majority four days later.
The fifth article penned by this writer since V-J day sounding the danger
signal over the legislative crisis ran in the Los Angeles Citizen under the head-
line "DRASTIC RESTRICTIVE BILLS INTRODUCED IN CONGRESS ON WAVE OF PLANNED HYSTERTA."
Shortly after President Truman vetoed the Case bill, another article was
written describing "the political program of Big Business". Appearing about July 4,
1946,, a month before the 79th Congress adjourned, it deplored the lack of "a strong
liberal offensive" to oppose "these corporate manipulators of our economy and of
our political life", declaring that "out of the NAM - fostered legislative program
• has come the Ball-Burton-Hatch bill and the infamous Case bill."
The 1946 general election neared, and on October 18, the challenge that
"The 'Big Steal' Boys must not win another battle!" was expressed in these words:
"The selfish business interests, flush with success over the
defeat of price control, approach the coming elections with
confidence, backed up by large campaign funds to support
candidates committed to voting for restrictive anti-Labor
legislation like the Case bill, passed by both houses at the
last session, and vetoed by the President.
failure of this political aspect of their formula depends
on the energy and effectiveness displayed by members of
Organized Labor and other liberal forces in the next few
weeks."

EDITORS
Fundamental Issues
In the Coal Strike
By Jons W. ScovuLLE
Consulting Economist
THE PRESENT COAL STRIKE brings to the sur-
face the flaw in popular thinking and legal
philosophy in regard to labor unions.
Recently I saw many miners in their blue
overalls walking and standing in the streets
of Kentucky towns. Obviously, no public
official has the right, or should have the
right, to compel anyone of these men to go
into the mines or to fine or imprison him for
not working. It is no crime to loaf or take
a vacation. Yet the public damage is so
great when all coal mines are shut down at
the same time, that there is a crime some-
where in the wood pile. Let us see if we can
find it.
===== PAGE 58 =====
WHERE THE GUILT WOULD BE APPARENT
Suppose that all owners of coal mines
formed a combination call it the United
Mine Owners-and in order to make coal
more scarce so they could raise the price
of coal, or to compel miners to accept lower
wages — should simultaneously shut down
all coal mines. This would be a violation of
the Sherman Act, and would subject the
mine owners to fines and imprisonment.
The mine owners have the legal (and
moral) right to form an association for
legitimate purposes, such as the collection
of statistics, presentation of facts to Con-
gressional committees, discussion of safety
measures, etc. But the association becomes
illegal when it seeks to limit competition
or restrain trade.
The objective of the Sherman Act is to
reserve competition and to outlaw monopo-
ies. Specifically, it would be illegal for an
isociation of coal mine owners to act in
ncert through the association to influence
: price of coal,— either to raise the price
lower it. Competition is superior (with
se exceptions) to monopoly as the regu-
r of our economic activities. For under
competitive system there is a tendency
ach enterprise to increase its profit by
asing sales and output, by offering bet-
sroducts, better services and lower
• This results in a national increase in
al output of goods and services, in a
higher scale of living, and greater material
prosperity. It causes trade and production
to gravitate to those individuals, firms and
areas that produce most efliciently. Monopo.
lies, on the other hand, tend to produce
opposite effects.
UNION DEPENDS ON MONOPOLY
Strangely enough, many people, and most
of the lawmakers, believe that workmen
should not compete with each other, but
should be allowed to form monopolies,
either industry-wide, such as the United
Mine Workers, or local, such as a labor
union in a single factory or mine. A labor
union in a single factory cannot be effective
unless it has monopoly power, which it en-
forces mainly through strikes.
We the people, acting through govern-
ment, are having difficulty in coping with the
coal strike. John L. Lewis says he did not
call a strike. To ask him to send the men
back to the mines is to admit that he has
monopoly power over coal mining. If we
force miners back to their jobs, that is in-
voluntary servitude. Our basic trouble is
that in our thinking, and in our laws, we
have condoned and encouraged the forma-
tion of labor monopolies. Those who believe
that labor monopolies should be protected
and encouraged by law should not complain
when labor monopolists shut down all coal
mines. If we sow thistle seed, we should not
complain when our fingers get pricked.
TAFT-HARTLEY ACT INADEQUATE
The Taft-Hartley law is not the answer. This
law seeks to eliminate some of the evils of
labor monopolies while still preserving
and protecting the labor monopolies. The
evil to be eliminated is not the logical and
inevitable effects of the monopoly, but the
monopoly itself. We have a double standard
of economic morals. The laws condone and
encourage monopolies formed by farmers
and workmen for the purpose of raising
prices, limiting output, and limiting compe-
tition, but brand as criminal associations of
industrialists which have similar objectives.
We may note that the monopolies which
are encouraged by government are among
groups of large numerical strength and vot-
ing power. Perhaps the conscience of the
lawmaker, when he ponders the monopoly
problem, is blunted by the fact that govern-
ment is itself a colossal monopoly, with
power to deprive the citizen of property,
liberty and life.
The fact that the majority rule in a
democracy should not obscure the fact that
the majority vote simply determines which
group shall be given monopoly power. In-
variably the ruling majority will pass laws
to benefit the ruling group, and to oppress
minorities. Thus, existing laws favor tenants
as against landlords; favor employees as
against employers; favor the poor as against
the rich. Those who clamor for greater
===== PAGE 59 =====
equality in wen, and income dent that
citizens should have equality in the lat.
Thus the employee ie free to quit the em-
ployer, but the employer is not free to die
charge the employee; the tenant is free to
vacate the bouse, but the landlord is not
free to evict the tenant; the tax rate for
the poor man is low, and for the rich man
is high. To expect that a majority of voters
will ever demand an impartial government
with equal rights for all is probably an
Utopian dream.
WHERE UNIONS HAVE FAILED
Many believe that labor unions are neces-
sary to increase the real wages of employees.
There is no conclusive evidence, I believe,
that labor unions have increased the real
wages of all who work,-and there is con-
siderable evidence that the reverse is true
that labor unions have lowered real wages.
But even if it could be proved that labor
unions have increased the real wages of
organized workers, this would be no valid
defense. We could not defend an association
of bank robbers by showing that the asso-
ciation had increased the loot. We cannot
solve our labor problems, or eliminate such
disasters as the present coal strike, until the
majority of voters realize that all monopo-
lies are evil,- and that competition will
result in more wealth-production, more jus-
Tice, more industrial peace, more prosperity.
than its opposite, which is monopoly.
We mast show the workingmen that if
they can organize a monopoly, other groups
tan do the same. That when all groups are
organized into monopolies, output will be
redaced and poverty increased. That in the
long run labor unions will decrease, and not
increase, the real wages of workmen.
But it is a monumental task to get people
to realize that liberty is desirable, and that
our economic affairs should be based on
voluntary agreements rather than on force.
violence and coercion-that business trans-
actions, insofar as possible, should be be-
tween individuals rather than between huge
groups of organized monopolists. The big
educational job that must be done is to
make the American people realize that in-
dividual freedom is superior to collectiv-
ism.
