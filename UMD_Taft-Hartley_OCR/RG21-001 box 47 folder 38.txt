Washington. D. C.
It is time again to look over the broad situation, to size up what is going
on in the world. Frankly, things don't look at all good.
A war attitude is guiding U.S. policy. There's a regular war strategy in
moves made.
The war at this stage is political, economic, not military.
War Itself, fighting war, is probably 10 years away, maybe 15, not 5.
Fighting war, however, could be the end product of present political war.
It is about all to look forward to. That's the long and short of it.
Idea of being tough with Russia, the "Truman doctrine," sounded good at the
time.
The only trouble is that Russia doesn't react according to formula. She
gets harder, not softer. That isn't what the theory called for.
Hard fact of the matter, with trimmings off, is simply this:
U.S. 1s Slipping, not winning, in the "cold" war, the political war. Troops
may have to follow dollars in Greece. That situation is bad. French Communists
have U.S. checkmated in carrying out ideas to put Germany to work. China is
Slipping from bad to worse fast. It really is a mess.
Marshall plan for putting up more dollars isn't doing so well either. Its
chance of success is not more than 50-50, if that. Europe wants all the dollars,
but doesn't want to make changes needed to become self-supporting.
congress,
too, is going to ask many questions before putting up more money.
Dollars, as matters stand, are not going to win the "cold" war.
If real war, "hot» war, then comes, it still won't be so simple.
U.S. probably cannot conquer Russia.
Top military men say that.
U.S. might, after immense effort, push Russia back to her old borders.
Europe, now in bad way, then would be a complete shambles, nothing left.
Russia would be very badly smashed up, too. There is no doubt about that.
U.S., in parts, might be quite a shambles as well--from bombs.
Also, man-power losses almost surely would be great. Debt now amounting to
$260,000,000,000 would be at least $1,000,000,000,000. Taxes would become con-
fiscatory if any effort were made to avoid real inflation. Another war will al-
nost surely end in socialism for U.S. The present system couldn't survive.
That's about the size of it. It is quite a problem.
Of course, it's easy to point out these things. It is not so easy to figure
out what would work any better, what course would avoid war.
Alternatives offered, but officially rejected, include these:
Alternative No. 1: Accept Russia's dominance in Eastern Europe.
Try to make
a deal on handling problems in the rest of the world. Recognize that Russia may
have a "sphere" in her area as U.S. has in this Hemisphere.
Alternative No. 2: Pull back from Europe and let the revolutions blow. For-
get about trying to shore up wrecked economic systems. Let new groups come to
power, even at the cost of wiping out old property rights.
Neither course is acceptable to U.S. It may be too late for either.
The U.S. dilemma, under present policy, basically is this:

FOREWORD
When presenting this pamphlet to the American People, a few
brief words concerning the history and origin of the two articles are
necessary. Part I entitled "A Letter to the 80th U. S. Congress" was
drafted and mailed to members of Congress and Secretary of State
George Marshall from the city of Miami, Fla. When it was read to my
local union—an A.F.L. affiliate in Pittsburgh, Pa.-T was informed,
by mail that it was well received and was approved by the member-
ship. I then mailed a copy to President Green of the A.F.L. who
promptly acknowledged it on behalf of the A.F.L. Executive Council.
Since then it has been widely read in liberal political and trade union
circles. Part Two is the text of an informal discussion between myself
and a group of trade union members, July 4th, 1947, in Pittsburgh,
Pa. This is the first time this text has been released to the public. For-
tunately, the reader will find it easy to check my remarks by looking
at the map of Europe prior to the rise of Hitler, and at the map as it
looks today, plus the reader's own experience here at home since
V-J Day. The actions of the G.O.P. controlled 80th Congress, in
enacting the Taft-Hartley Bill over the protests, not only of Organized
Labor, but business men as well, is but one example of how far men-
tally they are from the needs and wishes of the people. Since the Taft-
Hartley Bill is a clear violation of the personal and political rights
of all the American people, as guaranteed by the United States Con-
stitution, it must be repealed. Labor should leave no stone unturned
to accomplish the repeal of this infamous law and should now pre-
pare for the 1948 elections, and send to Congress men and women
who are mentally in step with the changed national and international
situation.
As to my identity- have been a member of Organized Labor
for a number of years, and it has been my good fortune to know not
===== PAGE 27 =====
only members of th
ade unions but business men, also. Through
my contact with them, I am convinced that only a tiny portion of
business here in the U. S. A. is reactionary, either in politics or eco-
nomics. My remarks on foreign policy will be helpful to those business
men who, in a few years time, will need a foreign market for the
surplus we will be producing. To the men and women who took part
in the discussion, I am indebted, as their remarks and financial help
was the inspiration needed to bring these facts to the attention of
the American people.

Honorable Members of Congress:
I sincerely hope that each and every member will give this letter
their most serious consideration, as it expresses some very decided
opinions about the future welfare of our nation. Since we all have a
the future welfare
personal stake in the U.S.A. the legislation enacted by the present
session, be it good or bad, cannot help but affect not only our country
but the rest of the world as well. I feel called upon to state my views
as an American citizen and as a member of the Organized Labor
movement for a number of years. I believe of the many important
problems our country faces today, two problems are of utmost impor-
tance and to the degree that Congress faces these problems in a realis-
tic manner the happier the U.S.A. and the rest of the world will be.
In number listed the two problems are as follows: (a) World Peace
and (b) Labor Legislation. Since Congress has already felt the pres-
sure of backward and unenlightened men who have been very articu-
late about the evils of the unions, it is high time that the real truth
was known concerning the activities of such bodies. Briefly I shall
attempt to call to your attention some old and new labor history.
Once you men and women know the facts then when called upon to
vote for or against repressive labor legislation you can do so with a
clear conscience and take the responsibility for doing so.

When the trade unions were first organized their objective was
to raise wages and shorten the hours of work for their members. On
the legislative field the union's efforts succeeded in having social legis.
lation enacted that benefited not only union men and women but all
sections of our population. The public schools and state institutions of
higher learning with which we point with so much pride to the rest
of the world are but examples of the efforts of the Unions in the early
history of our nation. Yet seldom is the fact mentioned that Labor
had to wage a bitter struggle against prejudice to have such legisla-
The United States Constitution and the Bill of Rights has at all
times had no better guardian than the Organized Trade Union move-
ment of the U.S.A. Space will not permit me to go into further detail
on constructive legislation of labor but 1 believe Congress should
review the period of 1920-1930 and if you do you will find that the
widespread use of the Federal Courts and the injunctions issued by
those bodies was rèsponsible for breaking strikes. Thus the power of
the Unions to bargain for better wages was greatly weakened, Doubt-
less the low wages paid by employers at that time was a factor in
helping to bring about the depression of 1929-1933. If you will but
remember, this was a dark period in American history with hunger,
homelessness and bankruptcy. No section of American life escaped
the effects of that awful period, and I am sure no member of Congress
wishes to be instrumental in causing fear and worry of another eco-
nomic disaster.
THE ROLE OF THE NATIONAL. LANOR RELATIONs ACT
Since 1936 this law by Congress has become very popular with
workers and its results are shown in the economic life of the country
as today the working people enjoy better wages and shorter hours
than ever before. It must be noted by Congress that ere certain em-
ployers would recognize the legality of such a law Labor had to plead
its case before the Supreme Court of the U.S.A.
Since then the Unions have grown in
bers and today many
elderly workers enjoy seniority and vacations with pay and dignity
of having a representative state and help to remedy any injustice or
grievance. Thus the unrestricted rights of unions

wise, the enemies of democracy will have a field day. Once the facts
are known to Congress it will be clearly seen that the role of the Labor
Unions today is much the same as when fint organized.

THE U.S.A. AND WORLD PEACE
Since I am not as familiar with world affairs as I am with matters
of labor I will confine most of my remarks to generalities as to the
course I think the U.S.A. should take as a leader for world peace
within the United Nations. The Senate will be called upon to ratify
treaties negotiated by the State Department and other matters such
as foreign trade, atomic energy, etc., and my remarks may be helpful
in arriving at some course of action. When the war came to a halt,
late in 1945, I read with awe the destructive power of the atomic
bomb. Since then I have had occasion to talk to several of my Union
brothers who witnessed the after-effects of the bomb on the two cities
of Japan. I could not help but be impressed with their views as well
as the testimony that the atomic scientists disclosed during public
hearings before the committees of the House and Senate. Since you
have records of the testimony I think it would be wise to use such
documents for a basis for some conclusions.
The open hearings disclosed three simple facts that must not be
lost sight of and I will list them in rotation as to their importance.
(a) That the secrets of the bomb could not be forever kept a secret.
(b) That there was no defense against an attack with atomic bombs.
(c) That atomic energy if properly used opened up unlimited possi-
bilities in all fields of American life and that with the natural re-
sources of the U.S.A. the average person could in a few short years
enjoy life as we had never done before. These are cold hard facts
and must be faced, as today, school children are asking how they can
be happy in an atomic world. Such questions must be answered by our
statesmen and leaders in public life. Mankind is faced with the prob-
lem of living in peace or destroying the better part of the civilized
world in an atomic war.

fact and remain mentally in step with the people of the country.
THE POWER OF ATOMIC ENERGY FOR PEACE
Unfortunately, the deadly power of the atomic bomb has over-
shadowed for a time the future use of atomic energy here in the
U.S.A. and within the United Nations as the common meeting
ground of the peace-loving nations of the world. I am also afraid it
has escaped the attention of some of our leaders in public life, who do
not yet seem to understand that when the war came to an end the
